# Paid ads (one-to-many, cold audience)

Renting other people's eyeballs: reach is guaranteed, returns are not, so paid ads are a game of efficiency. "All advertising works. The only thing that differs is how well it works." Do paid ads last of the Core Four: the other three build the skills and the cash this one burns while you learn.

## Making the ad (Part I)

The funnel from haystack to needles: pick the platform → target within it → craft the ad to repel everyone else → tell survivors what to do next. As the audience widens, efficiency drops but total profit grows (the book's example: $1,000 → $10,000 beats nothing, but $100,000 → $300,000 makes more money at a worse ratio). Goal: the biggest audience that still turns a profit.

### Platform checklist (all four must be true)
1. You have used it and gotten value as a consumer.
2. You can target your people on it.
3. You know how to format ads native to it.
4. You have the minimum spend. Start with one platform, and start studying its ads instead of skipping them.

### Targeting
Two in-platform methods, best combined: **lookalike audiences** built from your highest-quality lists (stack in order: customers, then warm outreach list, then cold leads, then engaged-but-unconverted ad leads) and **factor targeting** (age, gender, location, interests, timing) layered on top. More filters = more efficient and faster burned through; small specific audiences fund the expansion to bigger ones.

### The three elements of every ad
1. **Callout** (get noticed). The first impression is where the book claims a 20x difference; most testing effort goes to the first five seconds. Verbal callouts: labels ("Gym Owners", and for local ads the more local the better: "LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON"), yes-questions ("Do you wake up to pee more than once a night?"), if-then statements ("If you run over $100,000 per month in ads..."), ridiculous results. Nonverbal: contrast (bright, moving, attractive), likeness (people who look like the avatar, "quack like a duck", mascots never age and never renegotiate), the scene (show the yes-question visually).
2. **Value, via What-Who-When**. The What is the eight key elements: the four value drivers (dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, effort and sacrifice) and their four opposites (nightmare, risk of inaction, slowness of the current path, hard work ahead without it). Carrots and sticks. The Who multiplies angles through status: how the spouse, kids, parents, colleagues, boss, friends, rivals, and competitors will treat them differently. The When runs each angle through past, present, and future so the prospect feels the consequences of deciding or not deciding now. Long ads cover more angles, short ads fewer; the callout and CTA stay the same either way.
3. **CTA**. Tell them exactly what to do, quick and easy: obvious button, easy number, simple memorable domain. Strengthen with the scarcity, urgency, and reasons from the lead-magnet CTA rules.

### Capture the contact
Send clicks to a simple landing page matching the ad's look and language (no Frankenstein experience, continuity from click to close). Focus on the words and one image. Remind them of the action they just took and align the next one (consistency principle). Contact given = engaged lead.

## Money stuff (Part II)

### The three phases
1. **Track money.** Before spending a dollar, set up return tracking. Untracked ads are a casino played until you feel like stopping.
2. **Lose money.** Testing is buying data. The book's budget rule: let a test ad spend up to 2x the cash a customer pays you in their first 30 days, as long as it gets leads; kill it before 1x if it gets no leads at all. Expect many small losses; the wins are rare and huge, and the move is to 10x or 100x down on a proven winner.
3. **Print money.** Reverse the budget from the customer goal: customers wanted x CAC, padded 20% because scale erodes efficiency, divided into a daily spend. Commit to it. "If the number terrifies you, then you're doing it right."

### LTGP to CAC
LTGP = all money a customer ever spends minus all cost to deliver. The book's observed pattern (not a law): below 3:1 businesses struggle, above it they take off. Diagnostic: if your CAC is under 3x industry average, your ads are fine, fix the business model (raise LTGP); if over 3x, fix the ads. Winners and losers in a niche have similar CACs; they differ on LTGP. Costs approach zero; revenue per customer can approach infinity.

### Client Financed Acquisition
If a new customer pays back acquisition plus fulfillment within 30 days (the interest-free credit card window), scale stops depending on your bank balance. The book's worked example: $15/month membership, $10/month gross profit, $30 CAC, 3.3:1 lifetime, but two months to break even; add a $100 upsell taken by 1 in 5 (+$20 average per customer) and the first 30 days collect $30, making customers free. If the model cannot pay back in 30 days, engineer a bigger 30-day cash number: upsell immediately, raise the front-end price, or sell a paid lead magnet.

### Lessons
- Do not confuse sales problems with ad problems: if qualified leads with money are showing up and not buying, ads are fine.
- Your best-performing free content, and customer UGC, make the best paid ads nine times out of ten. Build a system that collects them.
- Never say "I'm not techy". The book: four years of saying it, reversed by four hours of focused effort.
- Onboarding move: search "how to place a [platform] ad" and place one for $100. You will be learning, not earning, and that is the point.
