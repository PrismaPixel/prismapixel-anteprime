# Lead getters: referrals, employees, agencies, affiliates (Section IV)

Lead getters are not a fifth method. You use the Core Four to get them; then they run the Core Four for you. Natural order of adoption: referrals → employees → agencies → affiliates. Highest leverage: a lead getter who gets lead getters.

## 1. Customer referrals

Referrals are worth more (buy more, stay longer, pay cash) and cost nothing. The referral growth equation: referrals in minus churn out. Referrals > churn and the business compounds with no other advertising; referrals < churn and advertising just refills a leaky bucket. The book's ratio math: at 4:1 LTGP:CAC, every customer bringing two more takes the blended ratio to 12:1.

Two reasons businesses get few referrals: the product is not as good as they think, and they do not ask. Diagnostic question: "Why are my customers too embarrassed to tell everyone they know about my product?" Goodwill = value minus price; word of mouth runs on it.

### Six ways to earn referrals with value (map to the parts of an ad)
1. **Sell better customers** (callout): find what the most successful customers share, and sell only people who match. The book's PR company case: narrowing to the 15% lowest-churn niche broke a years-long plateau.
2. **Set better expectations** (dream outcome): slowly lower promises until close rates dip, then stop; the gap becomes overdelivery.
3. **Get more people better results** (likelihood): survey for best results → interview them → find common actions → force new customers to repeat them → measure → match the guarantee's conditions to those actions. (Gym Launch: sale in the first seven days tripled LTGP, so a first-week sale became mandatory.)
4. **Faster wins** (time): a win is any positive experience. Deliver in small increments; updates are wins; customers form their lasting impression within the first 48 hours, so pack wins into it; BAMFAM: book a meeting from a meeting, never leave a customer in no man's land; pad timelines 50% and deliver early.
5. **Keep making the product better** (effort): monthly loop: find the most common problem → get the fix from customers who overcame it → improve → pilot with strugglers → roll out or iterate → next problem, forever.
6. **Tell them what to buy next** (CTA): after each big win, offer the next thing, or someone else will.

The one-customer thought experiment: if all future customers had to come from your one remaining customer, how would you treat them? Write it down, then do that for everyone.

### Seven ways to ask (asking works when the referral is itself an offer)
1. **One-sided benefit**: pay your average CAC to referrer or friend; ask for a three-way introduction (call, text, or email, not a name and number) right at purchase. Always ask for the spouse.
2. **Two-sided benefit** (Dropbox and PayPal): split the CAC between both parties. Dropbox 39x'd in 15 months; PayPal hit 1M users in two years [book figures].
3. **Ask right when they buy**, on the contract or checkout: "People who do our program with someone else tend to get 3x the results. Who else could you do this program with?" [Only use a stat like that if the user actually has it; otherwise drop the number, keep the ask.]
4. **Negotiation chip**: trade a discount for three introductions made on the spot; ethical because the terms changed.
5. **Referral events**: bring-a-friend or spouse-challenge promotions over one to four weeks.
6. **Ongoing program**: talk about the benefits of doing it with others in all advertising; the book cites a friend's 33% signup boost and 250,000 of 1,000,000 event tickets referred.
7. **Unlockable bonuses**: status, training, service levels, merch for referring plus a testimonial; cheaper than cash, can be two-sided.
Combo: a gift card worth one-third of program price for a friend, expiring in 7 to 14 days, delivered as a photo in a three-way text.

Baseline first: measure the current referral % and churn % before and after.

## 2. Employees

The internal Core Four: getting employees is advertising too (warm outreach = your network; cold = recruiting; content = job postings; ads = promoted postings; plus employee referrals, associations, staffing firms). "You get rich from what you make. You become wealthy from what you own": a business that needs you is a job, not an asset.

Training is the **3Ds**: **Document** (write the checklist by doing the job and recording it; test: could a stranger get your results following only the checklist?), **Demonstrate** (do it in front of them, adjusting the checklist wherever they stop you), **Duplicate** (they do it in front of you until the checklist reliably produces the outcome). If they fail while following directions, the directions are wrong; fix the checklist, not the person. Praise steps done right, no punishment during training, retrain the whole team whenever performance dips. Competence is not performance: knowing the steps precedes being fast; reps fix that.

Economics: total payroll / total engaged leads = cost per engaged lead; times leads-per-customer = CAC; hold the 3:1 LTGP floor. The book's example: a content team costing ~$100k/month producing ~30,000 engaged leads is ~$3.33 per engaged lead.

## 3. Agencies

Use agencies for exactly two things: learning new methods and learning new platforms. The trap otherwise: the senior rep moves to the newest client and your results decay. The book's script to open with:
"I want to do what you do in my business, but I don't know how. I'd like to work with you for 6 months so I can learn how you do it. Plus, I'll pay extra for you to break down why you make the decisions you do. Then I'll start training my team on it. Once they can do it well enough, I'd like to change to a lower cost consulting arrangement so you can still help us if we run into problems. Are you opposed to this?"
Run your team and the agency in parallel until your team beats them, then drop to consulting, then cut loose. Ten-point picking checklist highlights: referred by someone who got results, prominent clients, a waiting list, realistic expectations, long-term strategy not hacks, clear asks of you, regular updates in plain terms, a sound value-equation offer, and expensive ("all good agencies are expensive, but not all expensive agencies are good").

## 4. Affiliates

An affiliate is an independent business that tells its audience to buy your stuff. Six-step build:
1. **Find them**: "Who's got my leads?" What do your customers buy, where do they go, what do they use? Categories: softwares, products, equipment, services, groups, events. A business in several categories is a prime target.
2. **Offer them** a new income stream, advertised like any offer (callout to the owners, value template: more money from current customers, proven demand, no fulfillment burden, start tomorrow).
3. **Qualify them**: they invest before they promote. Make them a customer ("if they don't believe in your stuff enough to buy it, they probably shouldn't sell it"), an expert (paid certification), or both. Pricing benchmark from the book: charge 10 to 20% of what an average active affiliate earns in year one.
4. **Pay them** from your maximum allowable CAC (the one-part-in-four at 3:1). Three tiers: ~25% of max CAC for signing up, ~50% once activated, 100% once sustaining; the blended payout under max CAC quietly improves the ratio.
5. **Launch them** with Whisper-Tease-Shout: whispers of curiosity every 4 to 6 weeks until 60 days out, then every 2 to 3 weeks; teases revealing product, date, and value elements weekly until 14 days out, then twice weekly; shouts with bonuses, scarcity, urgency at least twice daily in the last three days, escalating to every thirty minutes at launch. Do the work for them: plug-and-play assets.
6. **Integrate them** so they never stop: easiest to hardest, they give your lead magnet away with every sale, they sell your lead magnet and keep the cash, they sell your core offer for a share, ideally paid forever and uncapped so they keep your customers alive.
Target from the book: get ten to twenty affiliates, work out the kinks, then scale; affiliate LTGP:CAC computed like any channel with 3:1 as the floor (the book's worked widget example lands at 12.5:1).
