# Cold outreach (one-to-one, cold audience)

Warm outreach without the warm: strangers do not know you and do not trust you, so you make up for it with targeting precision, personalization, big fast value, and volume. It is private (competitors cannot see it), platform-stable (the rules of cold email and cold calls have barely changed in thirty years), and brutally reliable once the numbers are known. Expect it to take months to a year to scale; the book's own team went from 0 sales in month one to 30/month in month nine.

Strangers create three problems, solved in order: no way to contact them, they ignore you, they are not interested.

## Problem 1: build the list

Targeting is the hidden advantage: you pick exactly who to contact ("hedge fund managers managing over $1B"). Three steps, most accessible first:
1. **Software**: subscribe to scraping/database tools, pull by criteria, test a few hundred contacts per tool for accuracy and responsiveness before buying in bulk.
2. **Brokers**: have multiple list brokers build to your criteria, test samples, keep the good ones.
3. **Elbow grease**: join the groups and communities where the avatar gathers; build the list by hand from directories and profiles. Slowest and freshest; "if you can search the database, so can everyone else."
Get the first 1,000 names together. More time than money: start at step three.

## Problem 2: what to say

Two levers, both needed within seconds:
- **Personalize (they don't know you)**: one to three researched details a friend might know, then a compliment tied to them. Makes cold look warm. Batch the research; do it yourself, hire it, or use software.
- **Big fast value (they don't trust you)**: "We're not trying to tickle their interest, we're trying to blow their minds in under thirty seconds." Lead with the offer or a lead magnet that is genuinely big: things people actually pay for, given free. The book's case: swapping a thinly disguised sales call ("game planning session") for as much free service as they could afford tripled take rates. If response is weak, up the ante until the offer is so good they feel stupid saying no.

Script rules: phone and chat scripts a page or two at most; cold emails half a page at most. No awards for prettiest script. Get 100 conversations or 10,000 emails done before tweaking anything.

## Problem 3: volume

- **Automate delivery**: record or template the message once (voice memo, voicemail drop, video, email template) instead of composing per lead.
- **Automate distribution**: dialers, senders, schedulers. Trade-off: the fewer leads that exist, the less automation you should use; tiny avatars (1,000 hedge fund managers) get full personalization, mass avatars can take templates. Allocate 10 to 20% of effort to testing brand-new tech.
- **Follow up, more times, more ways**: act like you are trying to reach your parents with something important. Email first so non-response becomes the reason to call ("I'm calling to follow up on my email"). Expect two to three conversations before a higher-ticket sale.
- **Recycle the list** after finishing it: wait three to six months and run it again. Schedules, attention, and circumstances change.

## Benchmarks (book figures, label them)

- Phone: 100 cold calls/day → ~20% pickup → ~25% take the lead magnet → ~4 engaged leads (about 1 per hour of dialing).
- Email: 100 personalized emails/day → ~30% open → ~10% of opens reply interested → ~3 engaged leads. Target ~3% of the list becoming engaged leads.
- DM with personal video/voice memo: ~20% reply → ~20 engaged leads per 100. Qualify with A-C-A (see warm outreach).
- Minimum health: LTGP at least 3x everything it costs to run the outreach. The book's portfolio example runs over 30:1.
- The book's worked team economics: reps at $15/hr plus $50 per shown appointment, 200 calls/day, ~2 shows/day → $120/show, ~33% close → $360 CAC against $3,600 profit, a 10:1 return. "Then you just add bodies."
- Reverse-engineer scale: 1 customer per 100 emails and a 100-customer goal → 10,000 emails/month → 333/day → 3 people sending ~111/day.

Track everything. Both of the book's failed cold outreach attempts ran without metrics; the one that worked tracked every stat.

## When to use

When you have more money than time, when the warm audience is exhausted, or when you want a channel competitors cannot see or copy. Delegate the doing as soon as the unit economics support a rep's wage.
