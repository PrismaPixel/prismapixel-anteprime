# Offers and lead magnets (Section II of $100M Leads)

All numbers below are the book's own examples and benchmarks. Label them "book benchmark" when they appear in a brief.

## Definitions

- **Lead**: a person you can contact. That is all.
- **Engaged lead**: a person who has shown interest in what you sell (opted in, replied, followed and reachable). "Engaged leads are the true output of advertising."
- **Offer**: what you promise to give in exchange for something of value.
- **Core offer**: the product or service in exchange for money. Rule: try advertising the core offer first. It might be all you need.
- **Lead magnet**: a complete solution to a narrow problem, typically free or low-cost, that once solved reveals the next problem, which the core offer solves. The salty pretzel at the bar: solves hunger, reveals thirst, the drink is the paid offer.

Standards: the lead magnet must be valuable enough on its own that you could charge for it, and consuming it should make the right people want more. "A person who pays with their time now is more likely to pay with their money later."

## The seven steps to an effective lead magnet

### 1. Pick the narrow problem and the person
Use the problem-solution cycle: every solution reveals the next problem. Pick a problem that is narrow and meaningful, whose next problem your core offer solves. (Book's own example: the book itself is a lead magnet; audience is businesses under $1M annual profit; core offer is Acquisition.com investing in them.)

### 2. Pick one of the three types
1. **Reveal their problem** (diagnosis). Best when the problem worsens with time. Examples: website speed test with money-lost estimate, posture analysis, termite inspection.
2. **Sample or trial**. Full but limited access to the core offer, capped by uses or time. Best when the core offer is a recurring solution to a recurring problem.
3. **One step of a multi-step process**. Give one valuable step free, sell the rest. Best when the core offer solves a complex problem. Example: first of three sealant coats free.

### 3. Pick one of the four delivery vehicles
Software (calculator, spreadsheet, tool), Information (course, guide, interview, event), Service (do work free: audit, adjustment, first coat), Physical product (chart, sample, book). 3 types x 4 vehicles = up to twelve lead magnets for one narrow problem. Make several versions and rotate them; rotation keeps advertising fresh and reveals the winner.

### 4. Test the name
Ogilvy: "When you have written your headline, you have spent 80 cents of your dollar." Five times more people read the headline than the rest. Test in this order: headline, then image, then subheadline. A better name can 2x to 10x engagement. Testing ladder: poll your audience if you have one; post asking people to reply 1 or 2; or just message people and ask. If people ask when they can get it, you have a mega winner. Small changes matter (the book's subheadline won by removing one word).

### 5. Make it easy to consume
Book benchmark: take rates and consumption can rise 2x to 4x from ease alone. Information in every format (video, audio, text, image). Services at more times in more ways. Software on every device. Physical products simple to order, fast to arrive, simple directions. The book cites $100M Offers consuming in a near-even quarter split across ebook, physical, audiobook, and video: multiple formats, multiple times the leads for the same work.

### 6. Make it darn good: give away the secrets, sell the implementation
The marketplace judges everything you offer, free or not. Goal: provide more value than the price of the core offer before they ever buy. Book rule of thumb: 99% of the audience will not buy, but they build or destroy your reputation based on the free stuff, so make lead magnets as good as paid products. Sucky fluff sends buyers and their referrals elsewhere.

### 7. Make the CTA easy and reasoned
A CTA gives (a) what to do, in clear direct words ("call now", not "don't delay"), and (b) reasons to do it now. Reason types, in the book's order of preference:
- **Scarcity**: honest capacity limits. "I can only handle five people per week." Fake scarcity destroys trust.
- **Urgency**: real deadlines. Promotions that actually end.
- **"Because" reasons**: any stated reason raises compliance (the book cites the Harvard line-cutting study); sensible reasons work best. "Because it's my birthday" still beats nothing.
Do it clearly and do it often. "Don't be clever, be clear."

## Lead magnet economics (the book's worked example, label as such)

Core offer direct: $10,000 profit per sale, $1,000 of ads per call booked, close 1 in 3 → $3,000 CAC.
With a lead magnet costing $25 to deliver: engagement rises, $75 of ads per call, $100 all-in per call; if 1 in 10 buyers → $1,000 CAC and a 10:1 return on the same spend.
Rule: even a lead magnet that costs money to deliver should lower the cost per customer. A $25 delivery budget out-values a $0 one; that is where experienced owners beat newbies.

## The four tests of a good lead magnet
1. Ideal customers engage when they see it.
2. More people engage than with the core offer alone.
3. Valuable enough that they actually consume it.
4. The right people become more likely to buy after consuming it.
